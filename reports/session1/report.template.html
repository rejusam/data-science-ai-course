Cohort report · Session 1

The distance between here and week 25

Twelve people started a Data Science & AI course on a Monday evening. Before anyone wrote a line of code, they answered twenty-five questions. This is what the answers say.

Dr Reju Sam John · Lead trainer Cohort 2026-08-03-DS-PT-AP-A Fieldwork 3 Aug 2026

The short version

The room wants, above all else, to build machine learning models and to work with generative AI. Those are also the two subjects it is least able to do anything with today. Nothing else in the data comes close to that gap.

That is not a warning sign. It is the most ordinary thing in the world on the first night of a course, and it is worth measuring precisely because it tells you what to teach, in what order, and what to say when somebody is thinking about quitting in week nine.

Three other things stand out. They underestimate how much of the job is cleaning data. When they get a question about causation wrong, they get it wrong in an unexpected direction. And what they fear will stop them is not the mathematics — it is their own calendars.

Finding 01

They want most what they can do least

Two questions, asked eight minutes apart. First: rank five capabilities by how much you want them by week 25. Second: rate your honest confidence in five subjects today, from never touched it to could teach it.

Read separately, each is unremarkable. Read together, they are the whole story of the course.

Second most wanted
34Rank score for building and evaluating machine learning models — beaten only by storytelling.
Second least confident
0.80Mean confidence in machine learning concepts. Four of ten have never touched it.

Generative AI is the sharper version of the same shape: third most wanted, and the least confident subject in the room at 0.60, with five of ten reporting they have never touched it.

The inverse holds too. Writing clean Python is ranked last of the five things they want, and it is the third most confident subject. People do not aspire to the thing they can already partly do.

The gap between what this room wants and what it can do is not a problem to be fixed before the course starts. It is the course.

Finding 02

The gap already has a timetable

Mapping each ranked goal onto the week it is actually taught turns an anxiety into a schedule. This chart is the single most useful thing to put in front of a student who is worried in week three.

The ordering is not an accident of scheduling, and it is worth saying so out loud. The top-ranked goal, telling a convincing story with data, lands in weeks 18 to 20 — near the end, because you cannot tell a story about a model you have not built yet. The lowest-ranked goal, Python, starts in week one, because everything else rests on it.

A cohort that understands why the thing they care about comes last is a cohort that is still there in week 18.

Finding 03

They think the job is the modelling

Asked what share of a working data scientist's time goes on finding, cleaning and preparing data, the room's answers ran from 20% to 100%. That spread is the finding. Ten people gave nine different answers, covering essentially the entire possible range: nobody in the room knows.

Seven of ten guessed below 80%. The direction of the error matters more than its size: a beginner who expects the work to be modelling will experience the first eight weeks of any honest data science course as a delay before the real thing starts.

It is not a delay. Modules 2 and 3 are about acquiring and cleaning data, and they come before any modelling precisely because that is the proportion the job has.

Finding 04

The revealing wrong answer

Four questions had a correct answer. Performance was reasonable at the two ends and weak in the middle, which is roughly what a week-one cohort should look like.

The correlation question is the interesting one. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise every summer; what is the safest conclusion? Half the room correctly identified that hot weather drives both.

Of those who did not, only one chose the punchline answer, that ice cream causes drowning. Four chose something else entirely: that the correlation must be a mistake.

But the correlation is completely real. It is strong, it is repeatable, and it will show up in any dataset covering enough summers. Dismissing a real signal because its obvious causal reading is absurd is a more expensive habit than the naive causal claim, and a much harder one to catch, because it looks like scepticism. It gets a full session in week six.

Finding 05

The obstacle is the calendar

Asked in their own words what was most likely to get in the way over the next 25 weeks, the room did not say mathematics, or coding, or the pace. Four said work. Two said time and one said lack of time. One said holidays. The remaining two named combinations: work and holidays, work and family commitments.

Every single answer was about available hours. Not one was about capability.

Asked what habit they would commit to, four of them said the same thing: practice. The others named revision, scheduling study time, and reviewing class recordings.

This is what part-time study is, and it is not a character flaw. It does mean the design of the course has to survive a bad week at work. Forty-five focused minutes on a weeknight beats one heroic Sunday, and the difference between those two patterns is the difference between finishing and not.

What changed because of this

A report that ends at "interesting" is not worth the time it took to write. Five things about how this course runs were decided or changed on the strength of these answers.

  • SetupEight of twelve are on Windows and three are on a shared or family computer. The setup guide is written twice, once per operating system, and nothing in week one assumes administrator rights.
  • First labThree had nothing installed and two did not know what was installed. The first hands-on session runs in the browser, so nobody is locked out by an installation problem.
  • GitSix of twelve had never heard of Git or knew the name only. It is introduced as a course-long habit from week one rather than assumed as prior knowledge.
  • SequencingThe roadmap in Finding 02 is shown to students directly, early, so the wait for the thing they want is legible rather than discouraging.
  • SupportHalf the room said their honest first move on an error is to read the error message; a quarter said they paste it into an AI assistant. Both get addressed explicitly, because the second one works right up until it doesn't.

What this cannot tell you

The findings above are worth acting on. They are not worth over-reading, and the reasons are ordinary enough to state plainly.

  1. Twelve people, and usually ten answering. Every percentage here moves by eight to ten points if one person clicks differently. Treat these as directional, not precise. A single percentage point carries no information at this size.
  2. Confidence is self-reported. The confidence scale measures what people are willing to say about themselves in front of a new cohort on night one, which is related to competence but is not the same thing. Both under- and over-statement are plausible here, in different directions for different people.
  3. Non-response is not random. Participation averaged 83%, and the Git knowledge question drew the fewest answers of all at seven of twelve. People are more likely to skip a question they cannot answer, which means the 86% correct on that question is almost certainly flattering.
  4. One moment, no comparison. This is a single snapshot with no baseline and no control. It cannot tell you whether this cohort is typical, and nothing here is causal.
  5. The 80% figure is a convention, not a measurement. It is widely quoted across the industry and it is useful for making the point. It is not something this analysis established, and it should not be cited as though it were a precise statistic.

Method. Twenty-five live poll questions delivered during the session of 3 August 2026. Twelve participants; mean participation 83%; most questions drew ten responses. The export was parsed from its original spreadsheet form into tidy tables, then aggregated. Rank scores are position sums across respondents. The confidence index is the mean position on a five-point ordinal scale from 0 to 4, weighted by how many chose each level.

Privacy. Aggregate counts only. No names, no individual response rows, and no study codes. The parser does not read the sheet containing participant display names, and a test enforces that.

Reproducibility. The parser, its tests, the notebook and the interactive dashboard behind these figures are in the course repository. Charts here were generated from the same tidy tables that feed the dashboard.
